# #104 Madagascar Long-eared Owl — *Asio madagascariensis* — v4 research

- Accessed: 2026-10-31
- Target: adult male, age-unknown older branching mesoptile, adult female
- Action: staged science revision of v3; v1–v3 retained
- Scientific status: false
- Name mapping: current *Asio madagascariensis*; historical original combination *Otus madagascariensis* is not the Madagascar Scops Owl.

## Evidence actually read/viewed

1. Rene de Roland & Goodman, “*Asio madagascariensis*” in *The Natural History of Madagascar* (2003), printed pp.1116–1117 and Fig.12.20. The chapter reports one 770 g female and says males are smaller, but gives no male mass or population ratio. At one studied epiphyte-tuft nest, two young moved around and off the nest at 36 days, used adjacent trees about 5 m away, stayed within 15 m during the following week and still returned for delivered prey; first feeding away from the nest was at 42 days. This supports an active, supported brancher, not unsupported flight or an exact universal schedule.
2. Frank Vassen, dated 4 December 2013, Andasibe juvenile photograph and metadata, Commons CC BY 2.0. The direct field image supports white/cream mesoptile, dark facial mask, short tufts, formed brown folded wings and a branch grip; exact age, sex, iris chronology and flight ability are not supplied.
3. Peter Day, dated 30 November 2016, Vakona Forest Lodge juvenile photograph, BirdForum. The image was opened at full gallery resolution: a white/cream fluffy older juvenile has a dark mask, short fuzzy tufts, grey-brown/dark-looking irides, well-formed brown barred wings and a secure branch perch. Exact age, sex, molt chronology and flight ability remain unknown.
4. Jean-Michel Fenerole, December 2006 Andasibe adult-male photograph, Oiseaux.net. The actual image was opened: adult field geometry shows long but narrow, flexible ear tufts rather than the broad fan-shaped v3 horns; orange iris, black bill, warm buff face and feathered lower legs are visible. The photographer's sex label is not independently measured, flash affects colour, and one posture cannot define all tuft positions.

## Actual v3 image audit

Opened the 1536×1024 archived v3 original, not only its run text. It passes species number/title, exactly three subjects, orange adult eyes, black bills, adult/juvenile stage separation, humid-forest context, juvenile two-foot support and near-equal adult display height. It needs a visual correction because both adults have extremely broad, rigid fan-shaped ear tufts, while the opened adult field image shows narrower flexible tufts. Adult toes are also exposed too continuously; the species direction is feathered tarsi/toe bases with only distal dark toe/claw ends visible. The v3 juvenile has legitimate formed wings, so v4 must preserve that stage rather than regress it to a newborn fluff ball.

## V4 design boundary

- Preserve the three-bird triptych and all labels, number and current scientific name.
- Narrow and soften both adult ear tufts; retain length and wide separation without turning them into tiny scops-owl tufts.
- Cover adult tarsi and proximal toes with warm buff featherlets; expose only distal dark toe tips and claws.
- Keep the juvenile age unknown, branch-supported and active, with white/cream down, dark mask, short fuzzy tufts and formed brown compact wings. Dark/grey-brown-looking irides are a rendering boundary, not a biological iris claim.
- Keep the adult female only modestly fuller/larger. One female mass plus an unquantified male-smaller statement does not authorize a fixed ratio.

## Unknowns retained

Exact juvenile age and sex, continuous molt sequence, iris chronology, flight threshold, population-wide adult size ratio, toe-by-toe feather boundary and universality of the epiphyte-tuft nest remain unknown. The concept therefore remains `scientifically_verified: false`.
