## Actually read this cycle

### DESERTOWL-BENDOV-2017
Amir Ben Dov, Aviam Atar, Erez Baruchi, Kochav Levi & Nir Sapir, “Breeding biology of Desert Owl in Israel,” *Dutch Birding* 39 (2017): 285–307.
- Institutional record: https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/breeding-biology-of-desert-owl-in-israel/
- Author-uploaded full text: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/320517643_Breeding_Biology_of_Desert_Owl_in_Israel
- Accessed 2026-11-07; pp. 298–305, especially Table 4 on p. 299 and plates 419–420 on pp. 300–301.
- Direct facts: eyes partly open about day 7 and fully open about day 9; wing flapping begins about day 20–25; young stand on the nest shelf at about day 25; first flight at about day 30; leave the nest site about day 34. The paper describes young first appearing on the nest shelf at c.25 days, moving along the cliff before final departure, and photographs c.30-day nestlings on the shelf and c.35-day fledglings.
- Design use: choose a supported c.25–30-day pale mesoptile juvenile on a broad sandstone shelf, open-eyed, with emerging but not fully adult wings and no airborne pose.
- Limitations: observations mainly two Israeli sites and small broods; exact individual age, iris chronology and feather-by-feather sequence remain uncertain. The source does not justify a newborn reconstruction or a universal cavity geometry.
- Confidence: high for recorded field events and stage/location; moderate for applying the approximate age window to a generic concept plate.

### DESERTOWL-KIRWAN-2015
Guy M. Kirwan, Manuel Schweizer & José Luis Copete, “Multiple lines of evidence confirm that Hume’s Owl *Strix butleri* … is two species, with description of an unnamed species,” *Zootaxa* 3904(1) (2015): 28–50.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.11646/zootaxa.3904.1.2
- Open author copy: https://nnhc.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/nnhc/files/15-zootaxa-kirwan_schweizer_copete_compressed.pdf
- Accessed 2026-11-07; pp. 28–50, especially Table 1 and pp. 41–42, Figs. 7–8.
- Direct facts: the widespread western population formerly called *S. butleri* was described as *S. hadorami*. Examined material shows no pronounced sexual dimorphism in size, wing formula or plumage, although females average slightly larger. Reported mass ranges were males 140.0–183.5 g (n=4) and females 214–220 g (n=2); sample is small. Colour varies individually.
- Design use: correct v2’s visibly larger male. Adults should be nearly equal in height and plumage; allow only subtle female fullness, not a fixed large size gap.
- Limitations: museum sample is small and geographically incomplete; mass is not a linear-height ratio. Do not translate it into a percentage body-size formula.
- Confidence: high for taxonomy and the authors’ no-pronounced-dimorphism conclusion; moderate for subtle visual fullness.
