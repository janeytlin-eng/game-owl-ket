## Evidence actually read

- `SNO-HOLT-GRAY-MAPLES-KORTE-2016`: Denver W. Holt, Kathy Gray, Michael T. Maples & Mark A. Korte, 2016, *Journal of Raptor Research* 50(2):131–143, DOI 10.3356/rapt-50-02-131-143.1. The article/abstract and methods were read. White protoptile covers hatchlings; dark-gray mesoptile begins replacing it around days 8–9; eyes open around days 9–11; by about day 14 the young are dark gray and primary quills emerge. Primary feathers erupt from sheaths around day 21; rectrix quills around day 25. Young leave the nest on foot near day 21; first flight attempts are much later, about days 36–43, and coordinated fledging about days 44–55.
- `SNO-ORI-2022GUIDE` (reused): Owl Research Institute photographic guide, p.13 flight-feather plate and Stage 4 description. Days 15–21 remain predominantly gray mesoptile, with primary feathers only beginning to erupt from sheaths; legs/feet become more feathered. This is a photographic secondary guide derived from the research program, not a substitute for the primary paper.
- `SNO-MIK2013-258-261`: Heimo Mikkola, *Owls of the World: A Photographic Guide* (2013), actual PDF pp.260–263 / printed pp.258–261. Adult male is mostly/plain white; female is spotted and barred and approximately 300–400 g heavier; both have bright-yellow eyes, blackish bill base hidden by feathering, thick white-feathered tarsi/toes and black claws. Downy chicks are grayish-white; mesoptile becomes dark gray-brown. The book uses historical `Nyctea scandiaca`; this project retains it as an old-name mapping and displays current `Bubo scandiacus`.

## Design boundary and unknowns

The shown young is a stage range, not an exact-day diagnosis: predominantly dark/mid-gray mesoptile, open eyes, compact low body, increasingly feathered legs/feet, both feet and belly/heels naturally supported on a tundra mound, with only short primaries beginning to erupt. It may move on foot near the top of the range, but the still image must not claim flight. Exact sex, exact day, iris progression, rectrix visibility, individual mass, universal nest-departure date and feather-count thresholds remain unknown. Science approval remains false.
